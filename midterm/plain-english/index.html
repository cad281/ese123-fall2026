Lesson 1

Units, charge and current

Current is how quickly charge moves, like water flow per second. Voltage is energy per unit charge; resistance opposes current. Conductance describes how easily current flows. Convert units before calculating.

I = Δq/Δt; V = E/q; G = 1/R k = 10³, m = 10⁻³, μ = 10⁻⁶, n = 10⁻⁹.
Worked example

A charge of 10 C passes in 5 s: I = 10/5 = 2 A. A 2 kΩ resistor has G = 1/2000 = 0.0005 S = 0.5 mS.

Common mistake / check: Milli and micro are different. Conductance is measured in siemens (S), not ohms.

Try it yourself

1. 500 μs equals how many seconds?
Show worked answer

B. 0.0005 s 500 × 10⁻⁶ s = 0.0005 s.

2. 6 C passes in 3 s. Find current.
Show worked answer

C. 2 A I = 6/3 = 2 A.

3. What is the conductance of 100 Ω?
Show worked answer

B. 0.01 S G = 1/100 = 0.01 S.

Lesson 2

Ohm’s law and power

A resistor turns electrical energy into heat. More voltage pushes more current through the same resistor. Power is the rate of energy use, not the total energy stored.

V = IR; P = VI = I²R = V²/R; E = Pt for constant power.
Worked example

12 V across 3 kΩ gives I = 12/3000 = 4 mA. P = 12 × 0.004 = 0.048 W = 48 mW.

Common mistake / check: Double the voltage across the same resistor and power becomes four times larger.

Try it yourself

4. 5 V is across 1 kΩ. Find current.
Show worked answer

C. 5 mA I = 5/1000 = 0.005 A = 5 mA.

5. 10 V × 2 mA gives what power?
Show worked answer

B. 20 mW P = 10 × 0.002 = 0.020 W = 20 mW.

6. Voltage doubles across a fixed resistor. Power becomes…
Show worked answer

B. 4 times P = V²/R, so the ratio is 2² = 4.

Lesson 3

Series, parallel and circuit nodes

Series means one path with the same current through the components and no branch between them. Parallel components connect to the same two nodes, so they share voltage. A node is a set of points joined by ideal wire; its drawn shape does not matter.

Series: Rtotal = R₁ + R₂ Parallel: 1/Rtotal = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + … For two: Rtotal = R₁R₂/(R₁+R₂).
Worked example

A 1 kΩ resistor in series with (6 kΩ parallel 3 kΩ): the parallel part is 2 kΩ, total is 3 kΩ. At 6 V, total current is 2 mA and the parallel part has 4 V.

Common mistake / check: Do not classify components by whether they look side by side. Trace the nodes.

Try it yourself

7. 2 kΩ and 3 kΩ are in parallel. Find equivalent resistance.
Show worked answer

B. 1.2 kΩ R = (2×3)/(2+3) kΩ = 1.2 kΩ, below either resistor.

8. Two resistors share both end nodes. What must be equal?
Show worked answer

C. Voltage Parallel branches have the same voltage because both endpoints are shared.

9. A 1 kΩ resistor is in series with (2 kΩ parallel 2 kΩ). Total?
Show worked answer

D. 2 kΩ 2 kΩ parallel 2 kΩ = 1 kΩ; then add 1 kΩ to get 2 kΩ.

Lesson 4

KCL, KVL and negative readings

At a junction, charge does not pile up in steady operation: current in equals current out (KCL). Around a closed loop, voltage rises balance voltage drops (KVL). A negative current means the real direction is opposite your chosen arrow.

KCL: ΣIin = ΣIout KVL: signed voltage changes around a loop sum to zero.
Worked example

7 mA enters a node; 2 mA leaves through one branch. The other outgoing current is 7 − 2 = 5 mA. A 9 V source with a 4 V drop leaves 5 V for the other series drop.

Common mistake / check: A negative number is often a direction or polarity result, not a broken circuit.

Try it yourself

10. 8 mA enters; 3 mA leaves one branch. What leaves the other?
Show worked answer

B. 5 mA 8 = 3 + I, so I = 5 mA.

11. A 12 V source supplies two series drops: 5 V and what?
Show worked answer

C. 7 V 12 − 5 − V = 0, giving V = 7 V.

12. A current calculation gives −2 mA for a right-pointing reference arrow. Meaning?
Show worked answer

A. 2 mA flows left The minus sign reverses the reference direction: 2 mA flows left.

Lesson 5

Voltage dividers and loaded dividers

In a series divider, the larger resistor gets the larger voltage drop. Output across the bottom resistor depends on its share of total resistance. Connecting a load across the bottom creates a parallel path and usually lowers that output.

Unloaded: Vout = Vin R₂/(R₁+R₂) Loaded: replace R₂ by R₂ ∥ RL, then use the same formula.
Worked example

12 V feeds top 8 kΩ and bottom 4 kΩ. Unloaded output = 12×4/12 = 4 V. Add a 4 kΩ load across the bottom: bottom equivalent = 2 kΩ, output = 12×2/(8+2) = 2.4 V.

Common mistake / check: The load goes in parallel with the bottom resistor; do not add it in series.

Try it yourself

13. 10 V feeds two equal series resistors; output is across the bottom. No load. Output?
Show worked answer

C. 5 V Equal resistors divide the supply equally: 10/2 = 5 V.

14. 12 V feeds top 4 kΩ and bottom 8 kΩ, no load. Output across bottom?
Show worked answer

B. 8 V Vout = 12×8/(4+8) = 8 V.

15. 9 V feeds top 3 kΩ, bottom 6 kΩ, and a 6 kΩ load across bottom. Output?
Show worked answer

D. 4.5 V Bottom equivalent = 6∥6 = 3 kΩ; Vout = 9×3/(3+3) = 4.5 V.

Lesson 6

Current division

Parallel branches share voltage. At that same voltage, a smaller resistance carries more current. Finding the common voltage first and using I = V/R always works.

For two branches: I₁ = Itotal R₂/(R₁+R₂) The other resistor appears because current divides inversely with resistance.
Worked example

3 Ω, 6 Ω and 9 Ω share 18 V. Currents are 6 A, 3 A and 2 A, adding to 11 A. Req = 18/11 ≈ 1.636 Ω.

Common mistake / check: Do not divide total current equally unless branch resistances are equal.

Try it yourself

16. 2 Ω and 4 Ω share a total of 9 A. Current through 2 Ω?
Show worked answer

C. 6 A I₂Ω = 9×4/(2+4) = 6 A; the 4 Ω branch carries 3 A.

17. 12 V is across parallel 2 Ω, 4 Ω, 6 Ω. Total current?
Show worked answer

B. 11 A Branch currents are 6 A, 3 A, 2 A; sum = 11 A.

18. Same voltage across two branches: one resistance is half the other. Its current is…
Show worked answer

D. Twice I = V/R: halving resistance doubles current.

Lesson 7

Resistor power ratings and worst cases

A ¼ W rating is a limit on heat dissipation under specified conditions. In series, current is shared, so a larger resistor with the same power rating reaches its limit first. In parallel, voltage is shared, so the smaller resistor reaches the same rating first.

Ilimit = √(Prated/R); Vlimit = √(Prated R) In series use the smallest current limit Rmin = V²/Prated.
Worked example

100 Ω and 200 Ω in series, each rated 0.5 W: limits are 0.0707 A and 0.0500 A. Use 0.0500 A. Maximum total voltage = 0.0500×300 = 15 V. Powers are 0.25 W and 0.50 W.

Common mistake / check: Check every resistor, not just total power. Calculated limits are ideal rating boundaries; real designs allow margin and temperature derating.

Try it yourself

19. 100 Ω rated 0.25 W: voltage at the rating boundary?
Show worked answer

B. 5 V V = √(0.25×100) = 5 V.

20. 150 Ω and 300 Ω in series, each 0.5 W: maximum total voltage (two decimals)?
Show worked answer

C. 18.37 V The 300 Ω limit is √(0.5/300) A. Multiply by 450 Ω: 18.37 V.

21. 12 V across a resistor: minimum resistance to keep power at or below 0.25 W?
Show worked answer

B. 576 Ω R ≥ V²/P = 144/0.25 = 576 Ω.

Lesson 8

Meters, open circuits and short circuits

A voltmeter compares two points, so connect it in parallel. An ammeter measures flow, so insert it in series. An open circuit is a broken path: no current through the opening, but voltage may remain. An ideal short joins two points with zero voltage difference, but can carry current.

Ideal voltmeter: infinite input resistance Ideal ammeter: zero resistance Open: I = 0 Ideal short: V = 0.
Worked example

An unconnected 1.5 V battery has essentially no external current, yet about 1.5 V between its terminals. A 0.20 Ω resistor plus two 0.10 Ω leads reads 0.40 Ω in a simple two-wire measurement. Four-wire sensing largely excludes lead voltage drops.

Common mistake / check: Power off and discharge capacitors before resistance measurement. Never connect an ammeter directly across a supply. A short is not the same as closing a normal loaded circuit.

Try it yourself

22. An open switch can have 5 V across it. Ideal current through the opening?
Show worked answer

C. 0 A An ideal open has no conducting path, so I = 0 even when voltage is nonzero.

23. Why use four-wire sensing for a very small resistance?
Show worked answer

B. To reduce lead-resistance error Separate voltage-sense leads draw almost no current, largely excluding current-lead voltage drops.

24. 1 kΩ ±5% allows what range?
Show worked answer

A. 950–1050 Ω 5% of 1000 Ω is 50 Ω, so the allowed range is 950 to 1050 Ω.

Lesson 9

Power supply: CV and CC

A current limit is a ceiling, not a promise to force that current through every load. In CV mode the supply holds the requested voltage. If that would demand too much current, CC mode lowers voltage to keep current at the limit.

Ineeded = Vset/R If Ineeded < Ilimit: CV If Ineeded > Ilimit: CC, I = Ilimit and V = Ilimit R.
Worked example

Set 8 V, current limit 2 A. With 3 Ω, requested current is 8/3 = 2.67 A, so CC gives 2 A and 6 V. With 10 Ω, CV gives 8 V and 0.8 A.

Common mistake / check: A CC indicator can explain why measured voltage is lower than the set voltage.

Try it yourself

25. Set 6 V, limit 1 A, load 2 Ω. Actual voltage and current?
Show worked answer

B. 2 V, 1 A At 6 V the demand is 3 A, above limit; CC gives 1 A and V=1×2=2 V.

26. Set 6 V, limit 1 A, load 12 Ω. Actual mode?
Show worked answer

B. CV at 0.5 A Demand is 6/12=0.5 A below limit, so CV holds 6 V.

27. A 2 A current limit means…
Show worked answer

C. Current is capped near 2 A The limit caps current; a higher-resistance load can draw much less.

Lesson 10

Oscilloscope divisions, cycles and frequency

A division is one large grid box. Vertical boxes measure voltage; horizontal boxes measure time. A cycle is one complete repeat: peak to next peak, not peak to the next valley. Period is seconds per cycle; frequency is cycles per second.

Vpp = vertical peak-to-valley divisions × V/div T = horizontal divisions per cycle × s/div f = 1/T.
Worked example

A wave is 3 vertical divisions peak-to-peak at 2 V/div: Vpp = 6 V. A cycle takes 4 divisions at 100 μs/div: T = 400 μs = 0.0004 s, f = 2500 Hz.

Common mistake / check: Match probe attenuation and channel settings. A 10× probe with the channel incorrectly set to 1× makes voltage read ten times too small.

Try it yourself

28. Peak-to-valley height 5 divisions at 200 mV/div: Vpp?
Show worked answer

B. 1 V 5×200 mV = 1000 mV = 1 V.

29. A cycle spans 4 divisions at 0.5 ms/div. Frequency?
Show worked answer

C. 500 Hz T=2 ms=0.002 s; f=1/0.002=500 Hz.

30. A zero-offset sine goes from a peak to its next valley in 1 ms. Full period?
Show worked answer

D. 2 ms Peak to next valley is half a cycle, so a full cycle takes 2 ms.

Lesson 11

Trigger: a repeatable starting condition

Imagine photographing a jumper every time they pass the same height while moving upward. The pictures line up even though the jumper keeps moving. Triggering similarly aligns repeated scope records; it does not stop the signal.

Edge triggering needs a level AND a direction Rising: crosses upward Falling: crosses downward Negative voltage can still be rising.
Worked example

Level +1 V, rising edge: 0→2 V qualifies. 2→0 V does not. −5→−1 V is rising because voltage increases. Several levels inside the signal range can work; there is no single magic trigger frequency.

Common mistake / check: If a −2 to +2 V wave is asked to cross +3 V, no valid edge occurs. Auto mode may still refresh and drift; Normal mode waits and may retain an old trace.

Try it yourself

31. Rising trigger at 2 V. Which segment qualifies?
Show worked answer

C. 1→3 V 1→3 V crosses 2 V upward. Other choices have wrong direction or never cross the level.

32. Falling trigger at −1 V. Which segment qualifies?
Show worked answer

B. 0→−2 V 0→−2 V crosses −1 V downward.

33. Signal ranges −2 to +2 V. Rising trigger at +3 V. Result?
Show worked answer

D. No crossing meets this condition The signal never reaches +3 V, so it cannot cross that level.

Lesson 12

Peak, offset and RMS

Peak-to-peak is the entire height from minimum to maximum. Amplitude is height from the centerline for a symmetric wave. RMS is the DC voltage that would produce the same average heating in the same resistor. Negative voltage still produces heat.

Vpp = Vmax−Vmin; offset = (Vmax+Vmin)/2; amplitude A = Vpp/2 for symmetric waves Zero-offset sine: Vrms=A/√2 Symmetric ±A square: Vrms=A Zero-offset triangle: Vrms=A/√3.
Worked example

For 8 Vpp with zero offset, A=4 V: sine RMS=2.83 V, square RMS=4 V, triangle RMS=2.31 V. A square spends nearly all its time at its maximum magnitude.

Common mistake / check: Do not apply the sine formula to every waveform. A wave centered at +3 V with amplitude 2 V has a maximum of +5 V, not +2 V.

Try it yourself

34. Zero-offset sine: Vpp=10 V. RMS (two decimals)?
Show worked answer

B. 3.54 V A=10/2=5 V; Vrms=5/√2≈3.54 V.

35. Square alternates +5 V and −5 V, each for 50% of the period. Total RMS?
Show worked answer

D. 5 V Both levels square to 25 V², so RMS=√25=5 V.

36. Symmetric sine: maximum +5 V, minimum +1 V. Vpp and offset?
Show worked answer

A. 4 Vpp, +3 V Vpp=5−1=4 V; centerline=(5+1)/2=3 V.

Lesson 13

Rectangular pulses and DC offset

Duty cycle is the fraction of each period spent high. A 25% duty cycle means high for one quarter of each cycle. Total RMS includes both the DC and changing parts; an AC-only meter reading may exclude DC.

D = thigh/T Total RMS = √[D VH² + (1−D)VL²] If VL=0: RMS=|VH|√D Average = D VH+(1−D)VL Offset sine: total RMS=√(VDC²+A²/2).
Worked example

A 0-to-5 V pulse high for 25%: RMS=5√0.25=2.5 V, but average=5×0.25=1.25 V. These are different measurements.

Common mistake / check: For a 0-to-6 V 50% pulse, total RMS is 4.24 V, not Vpp/2. State the two levels and duty cycle.

Try it yourself

37. 0-to-4 V pulse, 50% high. Total RMS (two decimals)?
Show worked answer

C. 2.83 V RMS=√(0.5×16)=√8≈2.83 V.

38. 0-to-8 V pulse, 25% high. Total RMS?
Show worked answer

B. 4 V 8√0.25=8×0.5=4 V.

39. Sine with DC offset +3 V and amplitude 4 V: total RMS (two decimals)?
Show worked answer

D. 4.12 V Total RMS=√(3²+4²/2)=√17≈4.12 V.

Lesson 14

Function generator: the internal 50 Ω

Model the generator as an ideal voltage source followed by a series resistance, often 50 Ω. If its display assumes an external 50 Ω load, it reports the voltage that load would receive. A high-impedance scope changes the divider.

Vload = Vsource RL/(Rs+RL) With Rs=50 Ω and a 50 Ω display assumption, Vsource=2×displayed value High-Z RL gives Vload≈Vsource.
Worked example

Display 1 Vpp with load setting 50 Ω: internal source is 2 Vpp. External 50 Ω receives 1 Vpp. A 1 MΩ scope receives nearly 2 Vpp because little voltage drops inside.

Common mistake / check: High-Z display setting usually changes the reported amplitude assumption, not the physical internal 50 Ω. Compare like units: Vpp to Vpp. Check the actual instrument settings.

Try it yourself

40. 50 Ω source, display assumes 50 Ω load, setting 2 Vpp. A 1 MΩ scope reads approximately…
Show worked answer

C. 4 Vpp Internal source is 4 Vpp; a 1 MΩ input receives nearly all of it.

41. Same generator, setting 1 Vrms assuming 50 Ω. Actual external load 100 Ω. RMS (two decimals)?
Show worked answer

B. 1.33 V Internal source=2 Vrms; load=2×100/(50+100)=1.33 Vrms.

42. Why can a scope read nearly twice the displayed generator voltage?
Show worked answer

D. Actual load differs from display assumption A high-impedance load draws little current, so little voltage drops across the internal 50 Ω.

Lesson 15

Capacitor charge, current and steady DC

Think of a capacitor as a water tank: charge is stored water, voltage is water level, and current is flow. A larger tank needs more water for the same level rise. This is an analogy: an ideal capacitor stores energy in its electric field; charge carriers do not cross its dielectric.

q = CV i = C dV/dt; for a constant voltage slope, i = C ΔV/Δt Positive current is defined entering the positive-voltage terminal.
Worked example

10 μF rises by 2 V in 1 ms: i=(10×10⁻⁶)×2/0.001=0.020 A=20 mA. Once voltage is constant, dV/dt=0 and ideal capacitor current is zero.

Common mistake / check: Use voltage CHANGE, not final voltage. With finite current, capacitor voltage cannot jump instantaneously. A real capacitor may have leakage.

Try it yourself

43. Ideal capacitor is settled at 5 V DC. Current?
Show worked answer

C. 0 A Constant voltage means dV/dt=0, so ideal current is zero.

44. 5 μF rises uniformly from 1 V to 3 V in 1 ms. Current entering its positive terminal?
Show worked answer

B. 10 mA ΔV=2 V; i=5×10⁻⁶×2/0.001=0.010 A=10 mA.

45. 2 μF at 3 V stores charge of…
Show worked answer

D. 6 μC q=CV=2 μF×3 V=6 μC.

Lesson 16

RC time constant and capacitor energy

Through a resistor, capacitor charging starts fast and then slows as the source-to-capacitor voltage difference shrinks. One time constant is a speed measure, not full charging time. Energy grows with voltage squared.

τ=RC VC(t)=Vf+(V0−Vf)e^(−t/τ) From zero at t=τ: VC≈0.632 Vf E=½CV² Same C: Enew/Eold=(Vnew/Vold)².
Worked example

R=10 kΩ, C=100 nF gives τ=0.001 s=1 ms. Charging 0→5 V: at τ, VC≈3.16 V; 1.84 V is still missing. Same capacitor from 3→9 V: voltage ratio=3, energy ratio=9, not 12.

Common mistake / check: The 63.2% rule applies to the change toward the final value. From 2→10 V, one τ gives 2+0.632×8≈7.06 V. After 5τ a simple RC step is about 99.3% complete.

Try it yourself

46. R=2 kΩ, C=10 μF. Time constant?
Show worked answer

C. 20 ms τ=2000×10×10⁻⁶=0.020 s=20 ms.

47. Charging from 0 toward 10 V through R. Voltage after one τ (two decimals)?
Show worked answer

B. 6.32 V VC=10(1−e⁻¹)≈6.32 V.

48. Same capacitor changes from 2 V to 8 V. Stored energy becomes…
Show worked answer

D. 16 times Voltage ratio=8/2=4; energy ratio=4²=16.
