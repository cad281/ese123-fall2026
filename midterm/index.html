Basics used in every lab

Ohm’s law and power

V = I·R I = V/R R = V/I P = V·I = I²·R = V²/R

Units: V in volts, I in amps, R in ohms, P in watts.

Percent error

% error = (measured − expected) / expected × 100%

Divide by the expected (nominal or calculated) value, not the measured one.

Prefixes

p 10⁻¹² n 10⁻⁹ µ 10⁻⁶ m 10⁻³ k 10³ M 10⁶

100 nF = 100×10⁻⁹ F = 1×10⁻⁷ F. Convert to base units before you calculate.

Lab 1

DC power supply, Ohm’s law, and the LED

A resistor gives a straight line on an I-V graph. An LED gives a curve, so it has no single resistance.

IdeaFormulaWhat it means
ConductanceG = ΔI / ΔVSlope of a graph with I on the y-axis and V on the x-axis. Unit: siemens (S).
ResistanceR = 1 / GFlip the slope to get ohms.
Differential resistanceRd = ΔV / ΔIUse two neighboring points on a curve. Small Rd means the LED is fully on.
Tolerance rangeRnom × (1 ± tol)1 Ω ±5% means 0.95 to 1.05 Ω.

CV and CC modes

The supply holds the voltage you set (CV) until the load wants more than the current limit. Then it holds the current at the limit (CC) and lowers the voltage.

Why the LED is a curve

Below about 2 V, almost no current flows. Above about 2.5 V, a little more voltage gives a lot more current, which is exponential.

From my report

Resistor slope = 1.003 S → R = 0.997 Ω (−0.3%). LED Rd: about 30 Ω at high current and about 570 Ω at low current.

Watch out (mistakes from my draft)
  • The slope of I vs. V is G, not R. Flip it.
  • Rd = ΔV/ΔI. I first wrote ΔI/ΔV.
  • Copy numbers from the table carefully. I used 0.000080 A instead of 0.00088 A, so I got 6410 Ω instead of 570 Ω.
  • An LED does not obey Ohm’s law.
Lab 2

DMM, KVL, series and parallel

Voltages around any closed loop add to zero (KVL). The DMM measures voltage across a part and current through it, and it slightly changes the circuit.

IdeaFormula
KVLΣ V around a loop = 0
Series sourcesV = V₁ + V₂ (same direction)
Series RR = R₁ + R₂ + …
Parallel R (two)R = R₁R₂ / (R₁ + R₂)
Parallel R (any)1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …
DividerVout = VS·R₂/(R₁ + R₂)
Lead resistanceRlead = (Rwith − Rnulled)/2

DMM loading

In voltage mode the DMM is a 10 MΩ resistor in parallel with whatever you measure. That doesn’t matter for small resistors, but with resistors in the MΩ range the reading comes out low.

Never measure R with power on

The ohmmeter sends its own small current. An outside source adds to that current, so the reading is wrong, and the meter can be damaged.

Ammeter in parallel = short

An ammeter has almost 0 Ω. Put it across a part and a large current flows, which can blow the meter’s fuse.

From my report

1 V + 2 V = 3 V, measured 2.99997 V. With R = 300 Ω, I = 10.047 mA (in the 9.52–10.53 mA range). 98.86 + 197.04 = 295.90 Ω vs. 295.71 Ω measured (0.06%). Each lead ≈ 0.109 Ω.

Watch out (mistakes from my draft)
  • Check the arithmetic: (295.90 − 295.71)/295.90 is 0.06%, not 1.21%.
  • 1.63 + 1.68 = 3.31 V = VS, so KVL holds.
  • Always write units. “0.6” alone is not an answer; 0.6 MΩ is.
Lab 3

Voltage dividers and LTspice

To solve a messy resistor network, collapse each group into one equivalent resistor, then use the divider formula.

Vout = Vin · Rbottom / (Rtop + Rbottom)
  • Vout is always between 0 and Vin.
  • The bigger resistor gets the bigger share of the voltage.
  • If a load is connected at Vout, combine it in parallel with Rbottom first. Vout drops.
  • A parallel combination is always smaller than its smallest resistor. Use this to check your answer.

LTspice reminders

  • .op gives DC node voltages and currents. .dc sweeps a source.
  • In SPICE, M = milli. Write Meg for mega: “1M” is 1 mΩ, and “1Meg” is 1 MΩ.
  • Every circuit needs a ground node (0).
From my report

3 V, 1 kΩ (top), 2 kΩ (bottom) → Vout = 2 V, I = 1 mA. On the board: 9.1k ∥ 13k = 5.35 kΩ (top), 22k ∥ 18k ∥ 27k = 7.24 kΩ (bottom), so TP1 = 5 × 7.24/(5.35 + 7.24) ≈ 2.87 V.

Lab 4

Function generator and oscilloscope

The generator has a hidden 50 Ω inside and assumes a 50 Ω load. Into a high-impedance load (DMM, scope, op amp) you get about twice what you set.

Vs = 2 × (setting) Vout = Vs · RL / (50 + RL)
  • RL = 50 Ω → Vout = setting ✔
  • RL ≈ 10 MΩ (DMM or scope) → Vout ≈ 2 × setting
WaveRMSExample: 1 Vpp (Vp = 0.5 V)
SineVp / √20.354 V
Square (50%, symmetric)Vp0.500 V
TriangleVp / √30.289 V

Reading the scope

V = (boxes) × (V/div) T = (boxes) × (s/div) f = 1 / T

10:1 probe

The probe divides the signal by 10. The scope must be set to 10:1 to show the true value; at 1:1 the reading is 10× too small.

Triggering

The scope starts each sweep at the same point of the trigger source, so that wave stands still. A wave from another instrument drifts.

From my report

Set to 1 Vrms. With only the DMM connected it read 2.005 V (+100%). With 50.13 Ω connected it read 0.996 V; expected 2 × 50.13/100.13 = 1.0013 V. The scope showed 2 Vpp for a 1 Vpp setting.

Lab 5

Capacitors and the RC high-pass filter

A capacitor blocks low frequencies and passes high ones. Its “resistance” (reactance) gets smaller as frequency goes up.

IdeaFormulaMeaning
ReactanceXC = 1 / (2πfC)Big at low f, small at high f
Corner frequencyfc = 1 / (2πRC)Where XC = R
High-pass gain(f/fc) / √(1 + (f/fc)²)0.707 at fc; ≈1 well above; ≈f/fc well below
High-pass phaseφ = 90° − arctan(f/fc)Output leads: 45° at fc, → 0° high, → 90° low
Phase from scopeφ = 360° × Δt / TΔt = time shift between the two waves

Three ways to measure

Counting boxes (least precise), cursors, and automatic measurements (easiest). They should agree within a few percent.

Low-level readings

At very low frequency the output is only millivolts, so noise makes the magnitude and phase unreliable. Say so in your answer.

From my report

R = 4.75 kΩ and C = 100 nF give fc ≈ 335 Hz. At 300 Hz: 1.29 V measured vs. 1.32 V theory, phase ≈ 45°. Board divider: TP2 = 5.079 × 150k/209k = 3.645 V; measured 3.63 V (−0.42%).

Watch out
  • The scope may show the phase as negative when channel 2 leads. Explain the sign in words.
  • Use f in Hz and C in farads: 100 nF = 1×10⁻⁷ F.
Lab 6

Operational amplifiers

With negative feedback, an op amp makes its two inputs equal (V+ = V−) and draws no input current. Every gain formula comes from these two rules.

CircuitGain
InvertingAv = −Rf / Rin
Non-invertingAv = 1 + Rf / Rg
Buffer (follower)Av = 1
Current senseVout = I · Rsense · Av
Feedback RC cornerfc = 1/(2πRfC)

Clipping

The output can’t go past the supply rails. A 741 on ±15 V swings to about ±13 V. If gain × input is bigger than that, the tops get flattened.

Sanity check

A swing larger than the total supply span (30 V for ±15 V) is impossible, so a reading like that means a measurement mistake.

From my report

Rin = 10 kΩ, Rf = 47 kΩ → −4.7. Measured 2.761/0.5775 = −4.78 (+1.7%). Board: 1 + 150k/4.75k = 32.58, and 0.07 Ω × 32.58 = 2.281 Ω, but I measured only about 0.14 Ω, so that stage needs checking.

Watch out
  • Inverting gain is negative. Keep the minus sign.
  • The non-inverting gain has the “1 +”.
  • A triangle wave’s RMS is Vpp/(2√3).
